The Long Way Round

By A. Writer

The ferry leaves the harbour at six in the morning, when the water is still the colour of slate and the gulls have not yet begun their daily argument over the fish market. Most passengers stay below with their coffee, but the view from the upper deck is worth the cold.

It takes four hours to reach the outer islands, and for most of that time there is nothing to see but the sea itself. That, the captain told me, is precisely the point of the journey, and after a while I came to agree with him. You can read more in the timetable.

The crossing

Halfway across, the wind turns and the boat begins to roll in long, slow movements that send the cups sliding along the tables. The crew have seen it all before and carry on with their work as if the floor were perfectly still beneath them.

Nobody takes this boat because they are in a hurry. They take it because they have stopped being in one.

By the time the first island appears as a grey line on the horizon, the passengers have fallen into the easy silence of people with nowhere else to be. Phones stay in pockets, since there is no signal anyway, and conversations start between strangers who would never have spoken on land.

  • Bring a warm coat, even in summer.
  • The café on board only takes cash.
  • Sit on the port side for the best view of the lighthouse.

The return journey leaves at four in the afternoon, and I have never once regretted taking the long way round. There are faster ways to travel, certainly, but none of them leave you feeling as though you have actually been somewhere by the time you arrive.
